\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Long-term memory must locate relevant history, preserve the conditions that make a fact trustworthy, and revise identities and beliefs when later observations arrive. Deep-Dream answers with a document-first temporal concept graph: concepts, relations, and versions form the address layer; append-only observations and source spans form the authority layer; and an agent controls evidence expansion.

This differs from summary memory, which commits information loss during writing; graph-only memory, which risks treating derived links as facts; and fixed full-context prompting, which spends the same large input budget on every query. The results show where the distinction matters: source reading helps multi-mention and update questions, relation links connect episodes, and deferred alignment reduces repeated ingestion work. They also show what remains open: graph links alone do not guarantee offline recall gains, deletion is not yet history suppression, and some temporal tasks remain difficult.

The broader contribution is a set of design questions for long-term memory: what is compressed for addressing, what remains authoritative, how identity is revised, and who controls the read budget. These questions provide a basis for future deletion, conflict, and evidence-calibration benchmarks.
